SECTION 1. INTRODUCTION AND VISION

PrepSuccess is a placement readiness and 
preparation platform built for college 
students who are approaching campus 
placements or off campus job hunting and do 
not have a clear, structured way to know 
where they stand. The product exists to 
answer one question for the student, phrased 
as simply as possible: where do I stand 
right now, and what should I do next.

Every year, large numbers of students, 
especially from colleges that are not tier 
one institutions, prepare for placements 
using scattered and disconnected sources. 
They watch YouTube playlists, follow random 
test series, borrow resume templates from 
seniors, and rarely get a single honest 
signal that tells them, in one place, how 
ready they actually are. PrepSuccess is 
built to be that single place.

The product starts intentionally small. The 
first release, referred to throughout this 
document and throughout the Jira project as 
Phase 1, is nothing more than a self 
assessment and a dashboard. A student signs 
up, fills a short profile, takes an 
assessment covering technical skills, soft 
skills, and aptitude, and is shown a 
readiness score broken down by category 
along with their weakest specific skills. 
That is the entire loop for the first 
release. Nothing about interview question 
banks, nothing about mentors, nothing about 
payments, and nothing about artificial 
intelligence is part of that first release.

From that small, validated core, the product 
grows in three additional phases. Phase 2 
adds an interview question bank organized by 
company, role, and topic, along with 
bookmarking and progress tracking over time. 
Phase 3 adds one to one mentorship, meaning 
a student can book a video call with a 
verified developer or mentor, chat with that 
mentor, and receive session notes that turn 
into concrete next steps on their dashboard. 
Phase 4 expands the platform to new user 
segments, namely freshers who have already 
graduated and working employees who want to 
benchmark themselves, and layers in 
artificial intelligence to generate 
personalized recommendations, resume 
feedback, and a downloadable personalized 
readiness report.

This document is the single, complete 
requirements reference for the whole 
project. It exists so that any engineer, 
designer, or reviewer can read one file and 
understand exactly what is being built, why 
it is being built in this order, how every 
piece of data and every screen fits 
together, and how the work is tracked day to 
day inside Jira. Everything described here 
has already been broken into epics, tickets, 
story point estimates, fix versions, and 
sprints inside the SCRUM project on the 
team's Jira site, and this document cross 
references those tickets throughout so that 
the plain language explanation here and the 
tracked work there never drift apart.

SECTION 2. THE PROBLEM BEING SOLVED

College students, particularly those 
studying subjects such as BCA and similar 
computer science adjacent programs, face 
three overlapping problems on the road to 
placement. The first is a lack of clarity 
about their own skill gaps. A student might 
feel generally unprepared without being able 
to say specifically that their data 
structures knowledge is weak, or that their 
resume is poorly structured, or that their 
aptitude test speed is too slow. The second 
problem is a complete lack of structure in 
how they prepare. Technical preparation, 
communication and resume preparation, and 
aptitude preparation are treated as three 
unrelated efforts, each pursued through a 
different source, with nothing tying them 
into a single readiness picture. The third 
problem is the absence of any real feedback 
loop. Students typically do not get an 
honest, structured signal on how ready they 
actually are until they are already sitting 
in an interview and failing it.

PrepSuccess addresses all three problems by 
giving every student one dashboard that 
answers the readiness question directly, by 
tying technical, soft skill, and aptitude 
preparation into one underlying data model 
built around a shared concept of a skill, 
and by giving the student a continuously 
updated feedback loop as they take more 
assessments, solve more practice questions, 
and attend more mentor sessions.

SECTION 3. WHO THE PRODUCT IS FOR

The primary and only user segment for the 
first release is the college student, 
specifically students in their last one to 
two years before placements or job hunting, 
starting with BCA and similar backgrounds. 
This is a deliberate narrowing of scope. 
Building for one segment first keeps the 
problem small enough to solve completely, 
and produces a foundation, meaning the 
assessment engine, the scoring logic, and 
the dashboard, that later user segments can 
reuse without a redesign.

Two additional user segments are planned for 
later phases. Freshers are people who have 
already graduated but have not yet landed a 
job and need continued guidance along with 
sharper interview preparation. Working 
employees are people who are already 
employed and want to benchmark their current 
skills, prepare for a job switch, or work 
toward a promotion. Both of these segments 
are explicitly deferred to Phase 4 and are 
not touched by any work in Phases 1 through 
3.

Beyond the primary student user, the 
platform introduces two additional roles as 
the phases progress. The mentor or developer 
role is introduced in Phase 3 and represents 
a verified professional who conducts one to 
one sessions with students. The admin role 
exists from the beginning conceptually but 
its concrete tooling, meaning the actual 
admin panel and admin APIs, is built 
alongside the Phase 1 release as part of the 
platform operations work, because admins are 
needed to approve mentors, manage the skill 
taxonomy, and view aggregate analytics as 
soon as there is real user data to look at.

SECTION 4. GUIDING PRODUCT PRINCIPLES

Every phase is expected to stand on its own 
as a usable product. Phase 1 by itself, with 
nothing else built, is a complete and 
launchable product: a student can sign up, 
take an assessment, and see where they 
stand. This means the team should never feel 
pressure to rush later phase work into an 
earlier release just to make the earlier 
release feel more complete.

Every phase's data model and infrastructure 
is designed in advance to make the next 
phase easier to build rather than harder. 
The clearest example of this is the Skill 
table introduced in Phase 1. It is designed 
from day one to be reused unchanged by the 
Phase 2 question bank, so that a question is 
simply tagged with a skill rather than 
requiring a second, duplicate taxonomy. 
Similarly, the User table introduced in 
Phase 1 is designed to be reused directly by 
the Phase 3 mentor and session tables, so 
that a mentor is simply a user with an 
attached mentor profile rather than a 
separate identity system.

Role based data boundaries are treated as a 
hard platform rule rather than a soft UI 
convention. A student never sees another 
student's data. A mentor sees only the 
narrow, session relevant slice of a 
student's data that is needed for that 
specific session, never the student's full 
assessment history and never another 
mentor's sessions. An admin sees aggregate, 
platform wide analytics and never browses an 
individual student's private results 
casually. These boundaries are enforced on 
the server, not just hidden in the 
interface, and every relevant ticket 
description in Jira repeats this requirement 
explicitly so it cannot be missed during 
implementation or code review.

Artificial intelligence, wherever it appears 
in the later phases, is designed to augment 
the existing dashboard and preparation 
material with personalization, and it is 
explicitly never meant to replace the human 
mentor connection introduced in Phase 3. The 
intended relationship is that AI provides 
scale and instant feedback, while mentors 
provide depth and human judgment, and the 
two feed into the exact same "next steps" 
list on the student dashboard so the student 
experiences one unified stream of guidance 
rather than two competing systems.

SECTION 5. TECHNOLOGY STACK AND REASONING

The frontend is built with Next.js. This 
choice was made because Next.js is a modern 
React framework with strong developer 
experience, straightforward deployment, and 
support for both statically rendered and 
server rendered pages, which suits a 
dashboard heavy application where some pages 
benefit from server rendering and others do 
not.

The backend is built with FastAPI. This 
choice was made because FastAPI allows fast 
development of REST APIs in Python, 
generates OpenAPI documentation 
automatically, has good asynchronous 
support, and integrates cleanly with 
SQLAlchemy for the database layer and 
Pydantic for request and response validation.

The database is PostgreSQL. The data in this 
application, meaning users, assessments, 
scores, questions, sessions, and messages, 
is fundamentally relational, with clear 
foreign key relationships between entities, 
so a relational database is the natural fit. 
PostgreSQL specifically was chosen for its 
maturity, reliability, and wide support 
across hosting providers.

Authentication supports both email and 
password and Google OAuth. This covers the 
two most common signup paths for students: a 
fast, low friction Google sign in for 
convenience, and a traditional email and 
password flow as a fallback for students who 
prefer not to use Google or do not have a 
Google account tied to their identity of 
choice.

SECTION 6. HIGH LEVEL ARCHITECTURE

The frontend, built in Next.js, never talks 
to the database directly. Every piece of 
data the frontend needs passes through REST 
calls, sent as JSON over HTTPS, to the 
FastAPI backend. The backend talks to 
PostgreSQL through SQLAlchemy. Google OAuth 
is handled entirely on the backend, meaning 
the Google client secret and the OAuth token 
exchange never touch frontend code, and the 
frontend only ever receives the platform's 
own issued session token or JWT once the 
backend has completed the OAuth handshake. 
This keeps all sensitive credentials server 
side and gives the platform a single, 
auditable point of control over 
authentication.

SECTION 7. PHASE 1, THE CORE MVP, DETAILED 
EXPLANATION

Phase 1 is tracked in Jira under five epics: 
Platform Infrastructure and DevOps, Auth and 
Onboarding, Student Profile and Assessment, 
Design System and UX, and Backend Foundation 
and Data Model. Its fix version in Jira is 
v0.1, Phase 1 MVP. The work is further split 
across three sequential two week sprints 
because the total ticket volume, roughly 
sixty tickets and around one hundred seventy 
story points, was too large to run as a 
single sprint. Sprint one is named 
Foundation and covers repository setup, 
environment configuration, database 
provisioning, both application scaffolds, 
the core schema tickets, and the design 
system tokens. Sprint two is named Core 
Build and covers authentication, profile, 
assessment, the dashboard, and the remaining 
wireframes and mockups. Sprint three is 
named Launch Prep and covers the admin 
panel, notifications, continuous integration 
and deployment pipelines, quality assurance, 
and legal and compliance pages.

The following paragraphs walk through every 
ticket in Phase 1 individually, in the same 
level of detail as the ticket itself carries 
in Jira, so that this document alone is 
sufficient to understand the full scope of 
the first release.

Ticket SCRUM-5, set up GitHub monorepo 
structure for frontend and backend. The goal 
is to stand up the GitHub repository that 
will host the entire PrepSuccess codebase as 
a single monorepo. The scope includes 
creating the repository with a frontend 
folder for the Next.js application and a 
backend folder for the FastAPI application 
as the two top level folders, adding a root 
README describing the repository layout and 
how to run each application, adding a root 
gitignore file covering Node, Python, and 
IDE generated artifacts, setting up branch 
protection on the main branch so that a pull 
request and at least one review are required 
and direct pushes are blocked, and defining 
a basic pull request template. The 
acceptance criteria are that the repository 
exists with both folders scaffolded even if 
they only contain placeholders for now, that 
the main branch is protected so that a 
direct push is rejected, and that a new 
teammate can clone the repository and find 
clear instructions in the README. This 
ticket blocks both the backend scaffold 
ticket and the frontend scaffold ticket, 
because both need the repository structure 
to exist first.

Ticket SCRUM-6, set up PostgreSQL database 
and hosting. The goal is to provision the 
PostgreSQL database that will back the 
entire platform. The scope includes choosing 
and provisioning a managed Postgres instance 
for development, using a provider such as 
Supabase, Railway, or Neon, creating both a 
development database and, if the free tier 
allows, a staging database, recording the 
connection string format along with a 
credential storage approach that supports 
safe rotation, and verifying connectivity 
from a local machine while confirming SSL 
mode requirements. The acceptance criteria 
are that a reachable Postgres instance 
exists with a documented connection string, 
that credentials are never committed to the 
repository, and that a basic connection has 
been tested using a client such as psql. 
This ticket blocks the SQLAlchemy and 
Alembic setup ticket.

Ticket SCRUM-7, configure environment 
variables and secrets management. The goal 
is to establish a consistent, secure way to 
manage environment variables and secrets 
across both the frontend and backend 
applications. The scope includes defining an 
example environment file for both the 
backend and frontend folders listing every 
required key, such as the database URL, the 
JWT secret, the Google OAuth client id and 
secret, and the API base URL, documenting 
the local setup steps of copying the example 
file and filling in real values, ensuring 
that actual environment files are never 
committed to git, and deciding how secrets 
will be injected in continuous integration, 
staging, and production environments, 
typically through a platform level secret 
manager. The acceptance criteria are that 
the example environment file exists in both 
applications with every variable documented, 
that no real secret value exists anywhere in 
the repository, and that a new developer can 
go from cloning the repository to running 
the application using only the README and 
the example environment file. This ticket 
blocks the email and password authentication 
ticket, the Google OAuth authentication 
ticket, and effectively every backend 
feature that reads configuration values.

Ticket SCRUM-8, scaffold FastAPI project 
structure. The goal is to scaffold the 
FastAPI backend application skeleton 
following the agreed project structure. The 
scope includes creating the main application 
file with application bootstrap, cross 
origin resource sharing configuration, and a 
health check route, creating the core folder 
for configuration and security helpers using 
pydantic settings, creating the database 
folder for the session and base model, 
creating the models folder, the schemas 
folder, the api version one folder, and the 
services folder, adding a requirements file 
listing FastAPI, Uvicorn, SQLAlchemy, 
Pydantic, Alembic, and either python jose or 
passlib for authentication related work, and 
adding a local run script or README section 
describing how to start the development 
server. The acceptance criteria are that the 
development server runs locally and the 
health check route returns a success 
response, that the folder structure matches 
the agreed project overview document, and 
that the automatically generated OpenAPI 
documentation is available. This ticket 
depends on the GitHub monorepo setup ticket 
and blocks every other backend ticket.

Ticket SCRUM-9, set up SQLAlchemy and 
Alembic migrations. The goal is to wire up 
SQLAlchemy and Alembic so that the backend 
can talk to Postgres and evolve its schema 
safely over time. The scope includes 
configuring the SQLAlchemy engine and 
session factory to read the database URL 
from the environment configuration, adding a 
declarative base class and a dependency 
function that provides a database session to 
FastAPI routes, initializing Alembic pointed 
at the SQLAlchemy metadata, and verifying 
that generating a migration automatically 
and applying it against the development 
database both work. The acceptance criteria 
are that a no operation migration runs 
cleanly against the development database, 
and that the database session dependency can 
be injected into a route and successfully 
execute a trivial query. This ticket depends 
on both the PostgreSQL provisioning ticket 
and the FastAPI scaffold ticket, and it 
blocks the core database models ticket along 
with every ticket that depends on a real 
schema.

Ticket SCRUM-10, implement core database 
models for User, Skill, Assessment, and 
AssessmentResult. The goal is to implement 
the Phase 1 SQLAlchemy models and generate 
the first real migration. The scope includes 
the User model with fields for id, name, 
email, a nullable password hash to support 
Google only users, a nullable Google id, 
college, branch, year, target role, and 
timestamps, the Skill model with id, name, 
and category as either technical or soft, 
the Assessment model with id, a foreign key 
to user, a type of technical, soft, or 
aptitude, and a taken at timestamp, and the 
AssessmentResult model with id, a foreign 
key to assessment, a foreign key to skill, 
and a numeric score, along with appropriate 
indexes and unique constraints such as a 
unique email on the User table and correct 
relationships so that, for example, an 
assessment object can navigate to its 
results in Python. The acceptance criteria 
are that all four tables exist in the 
development database with correct foreign 
keys, and that the relationships between 
them are navigable in code. This ticket 
depends on the individual schema design 
tickets for User, Skill, Assessment, and 
AssessmentResult, as well as the SQLAlchemy 
and Alembic setup ticket, and it blocks the 
authentication, profile, assessment, and 
dashboard API tickets.

Ticket SCRUM-11, authentication API for 
signup and login with email and password. 
The goal is to let a student sign up and log 
in using an email address and a password. 
The scope includes a signup endpoint that 
creates a user with a bcrypt hashed password 
and validates that the email is unique, a 
login endpoint that verifies the submitted 
credentials and issues a JSON web token as 
an access token, with a refresh token if 
that is in scope, password validation rules 
such as a minimum length along with clear 
error messages, and an authentication 
dependency function that protects other 
routes by requiring a valid current user. 
The acceptance criteria are that signing up 
twice with the same email returns a clear 
client error, that a valid login returns a 
usable JSON web token while an invalid one 
returns an unauthorized response, and that a 
protected test route rejects any request 
that does not include a valid token. This 
ticket depends on the core database models 
ticket and the environment and secrets 
configuration ticket, and it blocks the 
login and signup pages ticket on the 
frontend.

Ticket SCRUM-12, authentication API for 
Google OAuth login. The goal is to let a 
student sign in using their Google account 
instead of a password. The scope includes 
registering an OAuth client in the Google 
Cloud console and storing the client id and 
secret in environment configuration, a login 
redirect endpoint that sends the user to 
Google's consent screen, a callback endpoint 
that exchanges the authorization code, 
verifies the identity token, creates or 
finds the user by their Google id or email, 
and issues the platform's own JSON web 
token, and explicit handling of the case 
where an email already exists through a 
password signup, either linking the accounts 
or rejecting the attempt with a clear 
message. The acceptance criteria are that a 
brand new Google account that completes the 
flow ends up with a real user row and a 
valid session token, and that the Google 
client secret is never exposed to the 
frontend at any point. This ticket depends 
on the same two tickets as the email and 
password authentication ticket, and it 
blocks the Google OAuth login integration 
ticket on the frontend.

Ticket SCRUM-13, profile CRUD API. The goal 
is to let a logged in student view and 
update their own profile. The scope includes 
a route to return the current profile and a 
route to update college, branch, year, and 
target role, backed by Pydantic schemas with 
field validation. The acceptance criteria 
are that the route requires a valid token 
and rejects anonymous requests, that 
updating an unknown or invalid field returns 
a clear validation error, and that changes 
persist and are visible on the next read. 
This ticket depends on the core database 
models and the authentication API, and it 
blocks the profile setup page on the 
frontend.

Ticket SCRUM-14, assessment submission API. 
The goal is to let a student take and submit 
a self assessment covering technical, soft, 
or aptitude categories. The scope includes a 
start endpoint that creates an assessment 
row of the requested type, and a submit 
endpoint that accepts per skill answers or 
raw scores and persists them as assessment 
result rows, with input validation ensuring 
the assessment belongs to the requesting 
user and has not already been submitted. The 
acceptance criteria are that submitting the 
same assessment twice is rejected, and that 
submitted results can be queried per user 
and per skill. This ticket depends on the 
core database models and the authentication 
API, and it blocks both the assessment 
taking flow on the frontend and the 
readiness scoring API.

Ticket SCRUM-15, readiness scoring logic and 
dashboard API. The goal is to turn raw, per 
skill assessment results into the readiness 
score shown on the dashboard. The scope 
includes a scoring service that computes an 
overall readiness number on a zero to one 
hundred scale along with a category 
breakdown across technical, soft, and 
aptitude from a user's latest assessment 
results, a skill gap ranking that surfaces 
the weakest skills, and a dashboard endpoint 
that returns the overall score, the category 
breakdown, and the top gaps for the current 
user. The acceptance criteria are that the 
scoring logic can be unit tested in 
isolation from the HTTP layer, and that a 
user with no assessments yet receives a 
sensible empty response rather than an 
error. This ticket depends on the assessment 
submission API, and it blocks the readiness 
dashboard UI on the frontend, the future AI 
skill gap analysis service in Phase 4, and 
the admin analytics API.

SECTION 8. PHASE 2, INTERVIEW PREP, DETAILED 
EXPLANATION

Phase 2 is tracked in Jira under the 
Interview Prep epic, with fix version v0.2. 
Its dependency is that Phase 1 has shipped, 
specifically the Skill table, the 
authentication system, and the dashboard, 
since Phase 2 extends all three rather than 
replacing any of them.

The core idea of Phase 2 is that once a 
student knows their gaps from the Phase 1 
dashboard, the natural next step is targeted 
practice. The question bank lets a student 
search and filter interview questions by 
company, role, and topic, mark questions as 
bookmarked or solved, and see their 
improvement over time as a chart rather than 
a single static number.

Two new tables are introduced. The 
QuestionBank table holds id, title, body, an 
optional company, an optional role, a topic, 
a difficulty level, a foreign key to skill, 
and a created by field referencing the admin 
who authored it. This table deliberately 
reuses the Skill foreign key from Phase 1 
rather than inventing a second taxonomy, so 
a question about arrays ties directly back 
to the same skill that the Phase 1 
assessment already measures. The 
UserQuestionProgress table holds id, a 
foreign key to user, a foreign key to 
question, a status of bookmarked or solved, 
and an updated at timestamp, with a unique 
constraint across the user and question pair 
so a student cannot accidentally create 
duplicate progress rows for the same 
question.

On the backend, admins get exclusive create, 
update, and delete access to questions 
through dedicated routes, while students and 
the public get a read only, filterable, 
paginated listing along with a detail view 
for a single question. A separate pair of 
routes lets a student bookmark or unbookmark 
a question and mark a question as solved, 
both designed to be idempotent so that 
repeated calls never create duplicate 
progress rows, along with routes that return 
a student's own bookmarked and solved lists. 
A progress route aggregates a student's 
readiness score history together with their 
solved question counts over time, pre 
bucketed so the frontend can plot it 
directly without additional processing.

On the frontend, the question bank browsing 
screen lets a student filter and search by 
company, role, topic, and skill, with 
pagination or infinite scroll and bookmark 
and solved indicators shown directly on each 
question, and the filter state is reflected 
in the URL so a filtered view is shareable 
and bookmarkable in the browser sense as 
well. A question detail screen shows the 
full question along with inline bookmark and 
solve actions that update immediately 
without a full page reload. A dedicated 
bookmarked questions screen lists everything 
the student has saved with a remove action. 
A progress over time chart, shown on the 
dashboard, plots the readiness score history 
as a line or area chart alongside a 
secondary view of questions solved over 
time, and it is expected to render sensibly 
whether the student has only one data point 
so far or many. Finally, a downloadable 
curated prep PDF screen lets students browse 
and download admin curated guides such as an 
SDE interview guide or an aptitude quick 
reference, with the underlying PDF metadata 
managed by admins through the same content 
management tooling described in the platform 
operations section.

The acceptance criteria for the entire phase 
are that only admins can write to the 
question bank while students receive a 
forbidden response on any write attempt, 
that combining multiple filters at once 
works correctly, that bookmarking or solving 
the same question twice never creates 
duplicate rows, and that the progress chart 
renders correctly at every stage of data 
volume. Explicitly out of scope for this 
phase are AI curated or AI generated 
questions, which are reserved for Phase 4, 
and any concept of a mentor assigning 
specific practice sets to a student, which 
is not currently planned at all.

SECTION 9. PHASE 3, THE MENTORSHIP PLATFORM, 
DETAILED EXPLANATION

Phase 3 is tracked in Jira under the 
Mentorship Platform epic, with fix version 
v0.3. It depends on the Phase 1 User and 
authentication system, and it benefits from 
but does not strictly require Phase 2 to be 
complete first.

The reasoning behind Phase 3 is that some 
gaps, particularly around soft skills, 
career direction, and real interview 
experience, are best closed by a real person 
rather than by more content. This phase lets 
a student book a one to one video session 
with a verified developer or mentor, chat 
with that mentor around the session, and 
receive session notes afterward that become 
new, concrete next step items on the exact 
same dashboard surface that Phase 4's AI 
suggestions will also write to.

Three new tables are introduced. The 
MentorProfile table holds id, a unique 
foreign key to user, a bio, expertise tags, 
years of experience, and an approval status 
of pending, approved, or rejected. It 
deliberately extends the existing User table 
rather than duplicating identity fields, so 
any existing user can apply to become a 
mentor without creating a second account. 
The Session table holds id, a foreign key to 
the student user, a foreign key to the 
mentor profile, a scheduled at timestamp, a 
duration in minutes, a status of booked, 
completed, or cancelled, and a nullable 
notes field for post session follow up. The 
ChatMessage table holds id, a sender foreign 
key, a recipient foreign key, a nullable 
session foreign key, a body, a sent at 
timestamp, and a nullable read at timestamp.

The mentor lifecycle begins when any user 
applies to become a mentor, which creates a 
pending mentor profile row. An admin then 
approves or rejects that application through 
dedicated admin routes, and only approved 
mentors are ever included in the public 
mentor listing, which is enforced on the 
backend so that an unapproved mentor can 
never be discovered even if a student 
guesses a direct link. A mentor sets their 
own availability windows, with overlapping 
windows either rejected or merged rather 
than silently duplicated, and already booked 
slots are automatically excluded from what 
is shown as available.

Booking works through a dedicated endpoint 
that books an open slot, with double booking 
of the same slot explicitly rejected at the 
database or application level, plus a 
cancellation endpoint. A critical rule 
enforced here is that when a session is 
booked, the mentor is only ever shown the 
session relevant slice of the student's 
data, meaning their target role and key 
skill gaps, and never the student's full 
assessment history. This is the same role 
based data boundary principle described 
earlier in this document, applied concretely 
to the booking flow.

Video calling is handled through integration 
with a third party video provider such as 
Twilio Video, Daily, Agora, or a generated 
Google Meet link, with a join endpoint that 
returns a join token or URL that is valid 
only for the two actual participants in that 
specific session and only within a 
reasonable time window around the scheduled 
start time. Chat is handled through a 
WebSocket endpoint for real time messaging, 
backed by the ChatMessage table for 
persistence and a REST endpoint for fetching 
history, with the platform enforcing that 
chat is only ever possible between two users 
who already share a booking relationship, 
meaning this is never a general purpose 
social messaging feature.

After a session, the mentor can add notes 
and recommended next steps through a 
dedicated endpoint, and those notes appear 
on the student's dashboard next steps list 
within the same request cycle, with no 
separate manual synchronization step 
required. If the mentorship model requires 
payment, a payment integration creates a 
payment intent or order through a provider 
such as Stripe or Razorpay, with a webhook 
handler that verifies the payment signature 
and only then marks the session as paid and 
confirmed, meaning a session is never 
treated as booked until payment succeeds 
unless it has been explicitly marked as a 
free session.

On the frontend, a mentor profile page shows 
the mentor's bio, expertise, experience, and 
a preview of their availability with a book 
session call to action. A booking calendar 
screen shows the mentor's real availability 
as a calendar or slot picker, with a 
confirmation step and clear handling of the 
edge case where a slot becomes unavailable 
partway through the booking flow. A video 
call screen exposes a join call action that 
is only enabled within the valid time 
window. A chat screen shows the conversation 
list and message thread with real time 
updates delivered over the WebSocket 
connection. A session notes screen lets a 
student review notes and follow up actions 
from past sessions, and a payment checkout 
screen handles the payment step within the 
booking flow if payments are enabled, with 
success, failure, and pending states all 
handled explicitly so that a failed payment 
leaves the slot unbooked and retryable 
rather than stuck in an ambiguous state.

The acceptance criteria for the entire phase 
are that an unapproved mentor never appears 
publicly under any circumstance, that a 
mentor's view of a student is always scoped 
to session relevant data only, that double 
booking the same slot is structurally 
impossible, that a failed payment always 
leaves the slot available for a retry, and 
that chat is only ever possible between 
users who share an actual booking 
relationship. Explicitly out of scope for 
this phase are group sessions or webinars, 
mentor ratings or reviews, which are not 
currently scoped and would need a separate 
decision, and AI assisted session 
preparation, which belongs conceptually to 
Phase 4 rather than this epic.

SECTION 10. PHASE 4, AI AND PERSONALIZATION, 
DETAILED EXPLANATION

Phase 4 is tracked in Jira under the AI and 
Personalization epic, with fix version v0.4. 
It depends on the Phase 1 readiness scoring 
API for its input data, and it depends on 
Phase 3 having already established the 
shared next steps surface on the dashboard, 
since Phase 4's AI suggestions are designed 
to write into that exact same list rather 
than creating a separate one.

The purpose of this phase is to layer 
artificial intelligence assisted 
personalization on top of the assessment and 
mentorship data the platform has already 
collected, without ever replacing the human 
mentor connection from Phase 3. The stated 
guiding principle, repeated from earlier in 
this document, is that AI provides scale and 
instant feedback while mentors provide depth 
and human judgment, and the two are 
explicitly complementary rather than 
competing.

The skill gap analysis service takes a 
student's most recent assessment results and 
produces, through a large language model 
call, a plain language explanation of their 
weakest areas that goes beyond a bare number 
to explain why a gap matters and what to do 
about it. This output is strictly grounded 
in the student's actual scores, meaning it 
must never reference a skill that does not 
appear in the student's real data, and it is 
cached, regenerating only when the 
underlying assessment data actually changes 
rather than on every dashboard view.

The personalized next steps service combines 
that skill gap output with the student's 
target role to produce three to five ranked, 
specific, actionable next steps, which are 
written into the same shared next steps list 
that mentor notes from Phase 3 also 
populate, so the student always experiences 
one unified list rather than two separate 
and potentially conflicting sources of 
advice.

The resume feedback service accepts an 
uploaded resume, in at minimum PDF or plain 
text form, runs it through a large language 
model prompt tuned to the student's target 
role, and returns structured feedback 
covering which sections were found, specific 
issues, and suggestions, rendered as a 
scannable checklist on the frontend rather 
than a wall of free text. This feedback also 
feeds a resume quality signal into the soft 
skills readiness category on the dashboard, 
tying Phase 4 back into the Phase 1 scoring 
model rather than existing as an island 
feature.

The personalized readiness PDF service auto 
generates a downloadable document 
summarizing a student's current readiness 
score, category breakdown, top skill gaps, 
and AI suggested next steps, intended as 
something a student can review offline or 
share with a mentor ahead of a session. It 
regenerates when the underlying data changes 
and otherwise serves a cached version, and 
it is required to render sensibly whether 
the student has zero, partial, or full 
assessment data.

On the frontend, a next steps panel on the 
dashboard shows the AI generated items 
alongside mentor authored items from the 
same shared list, with loading and empty 
states while AI content is still generating. 
A resume upload and feedback screen handles 
the upload, shows a loading state while the 
AI processes the resume, and then renders 
the structured feedback as a checklist. A 
download button on the dashboard triggers 
the personalized readiness PDF download, 
with a disabled or explained state for a 
student who has no assessment data yet.

Explicitly out of scope for the currently 
ticketed work in this phase are AI assisted 
feedback on written or recorded mock 
interview answers, which the original 
product requirements document describes as a 
future, lightweight complement to human 
mentor sessions but which has not yet been 
broken into tickets, and AI generated or AI 
curated interview questions, which would 
logically extend the Phase 2 question bank 
but is likewise not currently scoped as a 
ticket.

SECTION 11. ADMINISTRATION, NOTIFICATIONS, 
AND PLATFORM OPERATIONS

This section covers everything that is not a 
student facing feature in its own right but 
is required to operate, govern, and safely 
launch the platform. In Jira this work sits 
under two epics, Admin and Notifications, 
and Platform Ops QA and Compliance, and the 
majority of it ships inside the v0.1 fix 
version alongside the rest of Phase 1, since 
admins, notifications, and basic operational 
safety are needed as soon as real users 
exist, not later.

Admin user management gives admins the 
ability to list, search, and filter both 
student and mentor accounts, deactivate 
accounts, and approve or reject pending 
mentor applications, all enforced through a 
server side role check rather than a hidden 
navigation link, meaning even a student who 
somehow discovers the admin route directly 
would still be rejected. Admin content 
management gives admins exclusive create, 
read, update, and delete access to the Skill 
taxonomy, the Question Bank, and the curated 
PDF library, with the explicit rule that 
deleting a skill or question that is already 
in active use is blocked or handled through 
a soft delete rather than a hard delete that 
would silently break historical data. Admin 
analytics exposes platform wide, strictly 
aggregate numbers such as signups over time, 
assessments taken, average readiness scores, 
mentor session volume, and question bank 
engagement, with a hard rule that no 
endpoint anywhere in the system ever allows 
an admin to browse a single student's raw 
results by casually clicking through the 
interface; the analytics surface only ever 
returns aggregates.

The notification system is backed by the 
Notification table described in the Phase 1 
schema section and dispatches both in app 
and email notifications, using a provider 
such as SendGrid or Amazon SES, for events 
including a session being booked, an AI 
suggestion becoming ready, or a mentor note 
being added. A core reliability rule here is 
that an email delivery failure must never 
block the action that triggered it, meaning 
a booking still succeeds even if the 
confirmation email temporarily fails to 
send, with the failure logged and retried 
rather than surfaced to the user as an 
error. On the frontend, a notification bell 
shows an unread count and a dropdown or 
panel of recent notifications, with toasts 
for real time events, and reading a 
notification updates the unread count 
without requiring a full page reload.

The DevOps work covers the full path from a 
developer's pull request to a live 
production deployment. A continuous 
integration pipeline runs linting, backend 
and frontend unit tests, and a build step on 
every pull request against the main branch, 
blocking the merge if any of those steps 
fail, with a target completion time under 
roughly ten minutes so it does not slow the 
team down. A continuous deployment pipeline 
triggers on every merge to main and 
automatically builds and deploys both 
applications to a staging environment, 
running database migrations as part of that 
deploy, with the explicit rule that a failed 
migration blocks the deploy entirely rather 
than leaving staging in a half updated 
state. A staging environment provides 
isolated instances of the backend, frontend, 
and database, seeded with representative 
test data such as sample users, skills, and 
questions, reachable at its own URL entirely 
separate from production data. Production 
deployment involves choosing real hosting 
for the backend and frontend, configuring 
production secrets, a custom domain, and 
HTTPS, and treating a promotion from staging 
to production as a deliberate, reviewed 
action rather than something that happens 
automatically on every merge. Monitoring 
integrates an error tracking tool such as 
Sentry into both applications, configures 
alerting to a real channel such as Slack or 
email for new error types and spikes, and 
adds basic uptime and health check 
monitoring for the API, verified by the rule 
that a deliberately thrown test error in 
each application must show up in the 
tracking dashboard within about a minute and 
must trigger a real alert, not just appear 
silently in a dashboard nobody is watching.

The quality assurance work includes unit 
tests for the authentication module covering 
signup with a duplicate email, password 
hashing, login with valid and invalid 
credentials, JSON web token generation and 
validation, and the Google OAuth callback's 
user creation logic, unit tests for the 
assessment and scoring module covering 
assessment start and submit, duplicate 
submission rejection, and the scoring 
service's overall and category calculations 
across edge cases including no data, partial 
data, and full data, with the scoring math 
specifically tested against hand computed 
expected values rather than merely checked 
for not crashing, unit tests for the Phase 2 
question bank module covering admin only 
write enforcement, filtering and pagination, 
and bookmark and solve idempotency, and unit 
tests for the Phase 3 mentorship module 
covering mentor approval, availability 
conflict handling, session booking including 
double booking prevention, and specifically 
that a mentor cannot see more student data 
than the platform's data boundary rules 
allow. End to end tests cover the signup and 
login flow from signup through profile 
setup, log out, log back in, and arriving at 
the dashboard, including a wrong password 
case, the assessment to dashboard flow from 
login through taking and submitting an 
assessment to seeing the correct score 
reflected on the dashboard, and the Phase 3 
booking flow from browsing mentors through 
viewing a profile, booking an available 
slot, and seeing it reflected in upcoming 
sessions, including the case where a slot 
becomes unavailable mid flow. A dedicated 
security review covers a dependency 
vulnerability scan for both applications, a 
manual review of authentication, role checks 
across student, mentor, and admin, and the 
enforcement of data boundaries described 
throughout this document, and a check for 
common issues including SQL injection 
through ORM misuse, cross site scripting, 
exposed secrets, and missing rate limiting, 
with the acceptance criteria that no high or 
critical dependency vulnerability remains 
outstanding and that a written summary of 
findings and fixes exists. API documentation 
work ensures every FastAPI route has a clear 
summary, description, and request and 
response examples, reviewed for completeness 
in the automatically generated 
documentation, with the acceptance criteria 
that a developer unfamiliar with the code 
can find and understand any endpoint from 
the documentation alone.

Rate limiting and API security hardening 
adds throttling on sensitive endpoints 
including login, signup, and the AI routes 
to prevent brute force and abuse, standard 
security headers including cross origin 
resource sharing locked to known origins and 
a content security policy where applicable, 
and a review of input validation across 
every endpoint for injection and oversized 
payload risks, with the acceptance criteria 
that repeated rapid login attempts are 
throttled with a clear error rather than 
silently allowed, and that cross origin 
resource sharing in production only allows 
the platform's own known frontend origin.

Finally, this section covers a handful of 
product and compliance tickets that are 
cross cutting rather than tied to one phase. 
A target role taxonomy definition finalizes 
the initial list of target roles offered in 
the profile form, such as SDE, Data Analyst, 
and QA Engineer, and maps each role to the 
relevant skills for scoring emphasis and, 
later, to question bank content, which both 
the profile setup page and the Phase 4 AI 
next steps service depend on. Terms of 
Service and Privacy Policy pages cover the 
data the platform collects, including 
assessment results, resume content, and 
payment information from Phase 3 onward, 
presented as static pages linked from the 
signup flow and the site footer, ideally 
with an explicit acknowledgement step during 
signup. Analytics and event tracking 
instrumentation integrates a product 
analytics tool such as PostHog, Mixpanel, or 
Google Analytics on the frontend and tracks 
key events including signup completed, 
assessment submitted, dashboard viewed, PDF 
downloaded, and session booked, with the 
acceptance criteria that each event fires 
exactly once per real occurrence rather than 
duplicating on a component re render.

SECTION 12. ROLE MANAGEMENT AND PERMISSIONS 
IN DETAIL

The platform has three core roles. The 
student role can take assessments and view 
their own dashboard only, book mentor 
sessions and chat with mentors they have 
booked or been booked by, and download prep 
PDFs along with their own personalized 
readiness PDF, and cannot see any other 
student's data, mentor internal tooling, or 
admin tools under any circumstance. The 
mentor or developer role sets their own 
availability for sessions, conducts video 
calls and chats with students who have 
booked them, sees only the session relevant 
slice of a student's data, meaning target 
role, key gaps, and prior notes with that 
specific mentor, and never the student's 
full assessment history or another mentor's 
sessions, leaves session notes and follow up 
actions that feed the student's dashboard, 
and cannot see other mentors' schedules, 
sessions, or students. The admin role 
manages user accounts for both students and 
mentors, including mentor onboarding and 
approval, manages the Skill taxonomy and 
Question Bank content, manages and authors 
curated interview prep PDFs, views platform 
wide analytics in aggregate form only, and 
handles moderation of chat or session 
reports.

A permissions summary, described here in 
prose rather than as a table, is as follows. 
Taking assessments is allowed for students 
only. Viewing one's own dashboard is allowed 
for students only, since mentors and admins 
have their own separate views rather than a 
personal readiness dashboard. Viewing 
another user's dashboard is never allowed 
for a student, is allowed for a mentor only 
in the limited session context described 
above, and is allowed for an admin only for 
support and analytics purposes in aggregate 
form, never as a casual browse. Booking a 
mentor session is something a student does, 
while conducting one is something a mentor 
does, and an admin does neither directly. 
Chatting, scoped strictly to an existing 
relationship, is available to both students 
and mentors, while an admin's only 
involvement is moderation. Downloading prep 
PDFs is available to students, unavailable 
to mentors, and available to admins only in 
the sense that they author and manage the 
underlying content rather than download it 
as a consumer. Managing the Skill or 
Question Bank content is exclusively an 
admin capability, unavailable to both 
students and mentors. Approving mentor 
accounts is exclusively an admin capability. 
Viewing platform wide analytics is 
exclusively an admin capability.

The operational rules that keep this 
trustworthy as the platform scales are as 
follows. Content management, meaning the 
Skill taxonomy, the Question Bank, and the 
curated PDF library, is owned entirely by 
admins and is never student or mentor 
editable, which keeps prep content 
consistent and vetted. Mentor vetting 
requires every new mentor or developer 
account to go through an admin approval step 
before it can appear as bookable, protecting 
the quality of one to one sessions. Data 
boundaries between roles are deliberately 
limited at every layer, meaning a mentor 
gets only what is needed for their specific 
session, a student never sees another 
student's data, and admins see aggregate 
analytics rather than browsing individual 
accounts casually. The feedback loop, 
meaning both AI suggestions from Phase 4 and 
mentor session notes from Phase 3, is 
deliberately designed to write into the 
exact same next steps surface on the student 
dashboard, so the student experiences one 
unified list rather than juggling separate 
AI and mentor recommendation systems.

SECTION 13. HOW THE WORK IS TRACKED IN JIRA

All of the work described in this document 
is tracked inside a single Jira project with 
the key SCRUM, on a board named SCRUM board, 
hosted on the team's Atlassian site. The 
project is organized around ten epics, each 
one representing a feature area rather than 
a single ticket, and every task level ticket 
in the project has been re parented under 
exactly one of these epics so that the epic 
hierarchy in Jira mirrors the section 
structure of this document.

The ten epics are Platform Infrastructure 
and DevOps, which covers repository setup, 
environment configuration, continuous 
integration and deployment, staging and 
production environments, and monitoring, 
Auth and Onboarding, which covers signup and 
login, Google OAuth, and the onboarding 
email flow, Student Profile and Assessment, 
which covers profile management, assessment 
submission, and readiness scoring, Design

everything needed to responsibly launch what 
was built in the first two sprints. Phases

One deliberate limitation exists in how work

SECTION 14. EXPLICIT NON GOALS ACROSS THE 
WHOLE PROJECT

Across every phase, a consistent set of 
things are explicitly not being built right 
now, and calling them out clearly here is 
meant to prevent scope creep during 
implementation. The platform does not 
support any user type beyond student, 
mentor, and admin at this time. There is no 
fine grained permission system beyond those 
three roles, and no concept of an 
organization or multi tenant admin 
structure. There is no support for group 
mentorship sessions or webinars, and mentor 
ratings or reviews are not currently scoped, 
meaning they would require a fresh product 
decision before any ticket exists for them. 
There is no AI generated or AI curated 
interview question content in the currently 
ticketed work, even though the question 
bank's data model would technically support 
it later. There is no AI assisted feedback 
on recorded or written mock interview 
answers yet, even though the original 
product requirements document describes it 
as a natural, lower priority future 
addition. Freshers and working employees, 
the two additional user segments mentioned 
as part of the long term vision, are not 
touched by any ticket in Phases 1 through 3 
and only enter the picture conceptually in 
Phase 4, without dedicated tickets of their 
own yet.

SECTION 15. SUCCESS CRITERIA FOR THE PROJECT 
AS A WHOLE

The project succeeds at each phase boundary 
if that phase's own explicit acceptance 
criteria, listed in detail throughout 
Sections 7 through 10 above, are met, and if 
the phase can be demonstrated end to end as 
a real, usable flow rather than a collection 
of disconnected screens and endpoints. 
Concretely, Phase 1 succeeds when a brand 
new student can sign up, complete a profile, 
take an assessment, and see an accurate, 
correctly scored dashboard with zero manual 
intervention from the team. Phase 2 succeeds 
when that same student can find, bookmark, 
and solve interview questions relevant to 
their weakest skills and see their 
improvement reflected as a trend rather than 
a single point in time. Phase 3 succeeds 
when a student can discover an approved 
mentor, book a real session, join a video 
call, chat around it, and see the mentor's 
notes turn into an actionable item on their 
dashboard, all while the platform's data 
boundary rules hold up under testing. Phase 
4 succeeds when the AI generated skill gap 
explanation, next steps, resume feedback, 
and personalized PDF are all demonstrably 
grounded in a student's real data rather 
than generic or hallucinated content, and 
when they visibly coexist with mentor 
authored guidance on the same dashboard 
surface rather than fragmenting the 
experience.

Underneath all four phases, the project as a 
whole succeeds if the core promise stated at 
the very beginning of this document remains 
true at every step: that a student can 
always come back to one dashboard and get an 
honest, structured, continuously improving 
answer to the question of where they stand 
and what they should do next.

SECTION 16. CLOSING SUMMARY

PrepSuccess begins as a simple self 
assessment and dashboard for college 
students and grows, phase by phase, into a 
full preparation ecosystem covering targeted 
practice content, human mentorship, and AI 
personalized guidance, all built on a data 
model that was designed from the very first 
phase to support everything that comes after 
it without requiring redesigns along the 
way. Every piece of scope described in this 
document has already been broken down into 
epics, tickets, story points, fix versions, 
and sprints inside the team's Jira project, 
assigned to real owners, and sequenced so 
that nothing later in the roadmap is started 
before the foundation it depends on has been 
built. This document is intended to remain 
the single, plain language source of truth 
for what the product is and why it is built 
the way it is, cross referenced at every 
section against the exact tickets that turn 
this description into working software.
